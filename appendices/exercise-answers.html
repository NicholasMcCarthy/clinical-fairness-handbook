Appendix C — Suggested answers to chapter questions

These are discussion guides. A defensible answer should explain its population, assumptions, and clinical use.

C.1 Introduction and background

  1. Similar accuracy describes recorded patients and their predictions. It does not measure unequal access to the service or people absent from the dataset.
  2. Evidence is needed about the changed action, uptake, benefits, burdens, and patient outcomes. A narrower statistical gap alone does not establish this.

C.2 Clinical applications

  1. When prevalence is very low, most negative results may be correct even if many of the few cases are missed. Sensitivity uses actual cases as its denominator; NPV uses negative decisions.
  2. Specify how limited appointments are allocated, how queues work, and what happens to people who cannot be served. Evaluate the resulting complete policy.
  3. Risk concerns an outcome under the observed or modelled conditions. Benefit concerns the difference caused by an action. They need different evidence.

C.3 Data and audit design

  1. Whole-dataset preprocessing uses information from evaluation data when constructing the fitted procedure, which can make evaluation optimistic or unlike the intended deployment process.
  2. The audit describes the included population with supported recorded group values. The number and characteristics of excluded patients, and the limitation this creates, should be reported.
  3. Establish whether spending represents the intended need or treatment benefit. Review access and measurement pathways rather than inferring suitability from predictive accuracy.

C.4 Modelling approaches

  1. Dichotomisation discards magnitude and introduces an additional threshold. Different cutoffs can produce different clinical questions and fairness results.
  2. A Cox linear predictor describes a relative hazard ordering. A horizon probability also needs baseline survival, time units, and a time origin.
  3. Independent thresholds can select multiple categories or none. A mutually exclusive outcome needs one joint rule.

C.5 Utility

  1. A constant probability can be calibrated at the cohort level while failing to distinguish patients with different risks. Calibration does not guarantee useful discrimination.
  2. Positive and negative errors can cancel. Compare absolute error, squared error, distributions, and relevant tails.
  3. AUROC does not specify the threshold, workload, clinical consequences, or value of action. The policy requires decision-specific evaluation.

C.6 Fairness

  1. No. Equal sensitivity can be equally low. Report both rates and counts and assess whether detection is useful.
  2. Prevalence differs. With equal TPR and FPR, Bayes’ rule still produces different PPV.
  3. There is no defined positive outcome, TPR, or FPR. A meaningful continuous-outcome criterion or a justified threshold-specific event must be stated.

C.7 Uncertainty

  1. PPV’s denominator is the number of positive decisions, not total patients or total observed cases.
  2. It preserves within-patient dependence by resampling the independent unit. A row bootstrap treats correlated encounters as independent information.
  3. It generally leaves out training and selection variation, measurement bias, unrepresented populations, and future shift unless the design explicitly models them.

C.8 Binary post-processing

  1. The source scores and their ordering are unchanged; only the decisions differ. Report score and decision metrics as distinct objects.
  2. It introduces an action probability and sampling variation. Reproducibility, repeated encounters, and clinical acceptability need explicit policies.
  3. Measure group coverage, reviewer capacity, delay, subsequent decisions, and outcomes for deferred patients. Retained performance alone omits that pathway.

C.9 Multiclass post-processing

  1. Each row is a conditional probability distribution over the one class that will be output.
  2. Argmax changes expected action frequencies and conditional rates. A constraint fitted for \(q\) need not hold for its largest-probability class.
  3. Include an unadjusted policy that samples from the original probabilities, alongside the original argmax baseline and adjusted policy.

C.10 Regression post-processing

  1. No. Prediction-distribution parity makes no general statement about the relationship between predictions and each group’s outcomes.
  2. \(\sqrt{0.25}=0.5\), so the original and fully transported predictions receive equal weight.
  3. It provides no guarantee for a particular fixed patient. The stated result is marginal over calibration and future observations within the declared group under exchangeability.

C.11 Survival post-processing

  1. The raw flag records whether an event was observed during available follow-up. A person censored early has unknown status at the chosen horizon.
  2. \(G\) describes the censoring distribution for IPCW; \(F_{\mathrm{ref}}\) describes event times for the CSD sampling approximation.
  3. It fixes other observed covariates and averages model evaluations. It does not identify all causal pathways or remove group differences in those covariates.

C.12 Other methods

  1. Survey weights support inference for a sampling population. Fairness weights alter an optimisation objective. Their assumptions and intended quantities differ.
  2. It does not specify acceptable false positives, missed cases, workload, or patient burden, and its numerical scale depends on the losses.
  3. A structural causal model, meaningful interventions, assumptions about pathways and background factors, and an identification argument are needed.

C.13 Worked cases

  1. “The EOpp gap fell from 20 percentage points to zero, while detected cases fell from 140 to 20 out of 200. Sensitivity is now 10% in both groups.”
  2. Evidence for all patients, including group-specific retention, reviewer actions and accuracy, waits, burdens, and subsequent outcomes.

C.14 Using CFT

  1. Supported confusion-based metrics and decision fairness comparisons remain possible. Probability calibration and ranking claims need appropriate scores.
  2. It means the computation completed. Clinical assumptions, applicability, and consequences are separate questions.
  3. Population and dates, endpoint and horizon, group definitions, threshold or policy, denominators, uncertainty, provenance, and relevant omissions.

C.15 Reporting and monitoring

  1. Clinician response, patient uptake, access, treatment effects, waits, and downstream outcomes may all remain unobserved.
  2. Patients with unobserved events may be incorrectly counted as non-cases, or the evaluated cohort may become selectively observed.
  3. Examples include worsened calibration, new missingness, increased false-positive burden, unequal waiting times, more deferral, or loss of outcome follow-up.
